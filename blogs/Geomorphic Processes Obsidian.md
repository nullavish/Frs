Geomorphic processes are the natural mechanisms that shape and alter the Earth's surface over time. These processes, driven by forces both, within the Earth and at its surface, continuously mold the landscape by creating, modifying and eroding landforms. From the powerful forces of tectonic activity and volcanic eruptions to the gradual effects of weathering, erosion and sedimentation, geomorphic processes are fundamental in the dynamic evolution of the Earth's topography. Through these processes, the Earth's surface is constantly being sculpted, leading to the formation of mountains, valleys, plains and various other landforms that define our planet's diverse and ever-changing landscape.

Geomorphic processes are categorized into EXOGENIC and ENDOGENIC FORCES, both of which play crucial roles in shaping the Earth's surface. Exogenic forces originate from external sources and include processes like weathering, erosion and sedimentation. These forces gradually wear down and reshape the Earth's surface. In contrast, Endogenic forces arise from within the Earth, driven by its internal energy. These forces include tectonic movements, volcanic activity and earthquakes, which create and uplift landforms, contributing to the dynamic nature of the Earth's topography. Together, exogenic and endogenic forces interact to continually sculpt and transform the landscape.

![Geomorphic Processes – Figure 1](https://cdn-mineru.openxlab.org.cn/result/2026-09-02/d3afdc39-d85b-4dcd-bb42-06e7df48cde5/aaf6350d0b7e75f84c9d2daa44effaa8c9fd727328b259a0dccb64579c4cbee2.jpg)

Endogenic processes are internal forces that originate from within the Earth's interior and are responsible for creating and modifying the Earth's surface. These processes are powered by the Earth's internal heat, generated from radioactive decay, gravitational forces and the residual heat from the Earth's formation. The energy from these processes drives the movement of tectonic plates, causing significant changes in landforms.

Endogenic forces contribute to the uplift of landforms such as the formation of mountain ranges through processes like orogeny, which involves the collision and compression of tectonic plates. Due to variations in geothermal gradients and heat flow from within, crustal thickness and strength, the action of endogenic forces are not uniform and hence the tectonically controlled original crustal surface is uneven.

Endogenic processes are primarily classified into : ‘ Diastrophic ’ and ‘ Catastrophic ’ processes .

## Diastrophism (Slow Movement)

Diastrophism Encompasses All Processes That Move, Elevate Or Build Up Sections Of The Earth'S Crust. This Includes All Movements Of The Earth'S Crust Caused By Tectonic Forces. It Involves The Deformation Of The Earth'S Crust Through Movements Such As Folding, Faulting, Warping And Fracturing. Diastrophism Is Responsible For Large-Scale Deformation, Which Leads To The Formation Of Structural Features Such As Mountains, Plateaus And Rift Valleys.

Diastrophic movements can be further classified into :

- Orogenic <font color="#2DC26B">Processes</font> : leads to the formation of mountains

- Epeirogenic <font color="#2DC26B">Processes</font> : leads to the formation of continent

## Orogenic Processes

Orogenic movements are part of the larger process of diastrophism, which refers to all movements of the Earth's crust caused by internal forces. These movements deform the Earth's

surface, leading to the formation of various landforms. Orogenic movements specifically involve horizontal compressive forces that result in mountain ranges.

- These movements are largely horizontal, as tectonic plates collide and push against each other.

- Orogenic processes are much more intense than epeirogenic movements, which are more vertical and gentle.

- The intense compression causes folding (bending of rock layers) and faulting (breaking and displacement of rocks) in the Earth's crust.

- Orogenic movements result in the uplift of mountain ranges, such as the Himalayas, Alps, and Rockies.

## Epeirogenic Processes

Epeirogenic Movements refer to large-scale, gradual uplift or subsidence of land masses, often affecting continents or large portions of the Earth's crust. Unlike orogenic (mountain-building) movements, epeirogenic movements do not lead to folding or intense deformation of the crust.

- These movements are also termed as continental or crustal movements and are typically vertical in nature resulting in the uplift or subsidence of land.

- These movements are gradual and take place over a long period typically affecting larger areas, such as whole continents or continental margins.

- One of the common causes of epeirogenic movements is isostatic adjustment, where the Earth's crust compensates for changes in load

- For Example : The upliftment of the Deccan Plateau

### NOTE :

- ISOSTASY : concept explains the balance between the Earth's lithosphere and the asthenosphere. The changes in load, such as melting glaciers, deposition of sediments, or erosion, can cause the land to rise (uplift) or sink (subsidence).

### CATASTROPHISM ( SUDDEN MOVEMENTS )

Sudden movements, in contrast to diastrophism, are rapid and short-lived geomorphic processes caused by the release of accumulated energy within the Earth's crust. These movements result in natural hazards like earthquakes, volcanic eruptions and landslides. Though short in duration, they cause significant and often destructive changes to the Earth's surface.

- EARTHQUAKES : Earthquakes are caused by the sudden release of energy due to the movement of tectonic plates along faults or fractures in the Earth's crust. Earthquakes can result in the rupturing of the Earth's surface, creating faults and other deformational features.

- VOLCANIC ERUPTIONS : Volcanic activity occurs when magma from the mantle reaches the Earth's surface through vents, leading to the formation of volcanoes. Volcanic eruptions can reshape landscapes by depositing lava, ash and volcanic rocks over large areas.

### MOUNTAIN ( OROGENIC ) BUILDING

Orogenic or mountain-building processes are closely linked to tectonic activity, where large-scale forces cause deformation of the Earth's crust. These forces result in various types of faults, which are fractures or zones of weakness where rocks have slipped past each other. Reverse fault and normal fault are the key fault types that contribute to mountain formation. Each of these faults is associated with different stress regimes and plays a crucial role in shaping mountain ranges by either compressing or stretching the Earth's crust.

![Geomorphic Processes – Figure 1](https://cdn-mineru.openxlab.org.cn/result/2026-09-02/d3afdc39-d85b-4dcd-bb42-06e7df48cde5/782debc570994edbae68827007ea46dc789b6a3a6f37947232518b831ee8d907.jpg)

### REVERSE FAULT

A reverse fault occurs when the crust is compressed, causing one block of rock to move upward relative to the other. Reverse faults are common in convergent plate boundaries, where tectonic plates collide. This type of fault is responsible for the compression and folding of rock layers, a primary mechanism in the formation of fold mountains.

When studying mountain building under compressional forces or in association with reverse faults, it's essential to understand the variety of folds formed due to crustal deformation. Folds are bent or curved rock layers formed when compressional forces act on ductile rocks, causing them to buckle instead of breaking.

### ▶ ANTICLINE AND SYNCLINE FOLD :

These are the most common types of folds formed by compressional forces during mountain building. They are typically seen together in folded mountain ranges.

- An anticline is an upward-arching fold where rock layers are bent upward in a convex shape.

○ A syncline is a downward-arching fold where rock layers are bent downward, forming a concave shape.

![Geomorphic Processes – Figure 1](https://cdn-mineru.openxlab.org.cn/result/2026-09-02/d3afdc39-d85b-4dcd-bb42-06e7df48cde5/265a4076ffe436e73788712d0486fd545f7cf10b866c82c7e7bd37dc88d888e8.jpg)

### SYMMETRICAL FOLD :

○ In a symmetrical fold, both limbs of the fold are mirror images of each other, and the axial plane (the imaginary plane that divides the fold) is vertical.

- This type of fold results when the compressional forces act evenly on both sides.

### ASYMMETRICAL FOLD :

○ An asymmetrical fold occurs when one limb of the fold is steeper than the other, and the axial plane is inclined.

This type of fold results when compressional forces are uneven, causing one side of the fold to tilt more than the other.

### ➤ OVERTURNED FOLD :

![Geomorphic Processes – Figure 1](https://cdn-mineru.openxlab.org.cn/result/2026-09-02/d3afdc39-d85b-4dcd-bb42-06e7df48cde5/11656ec41a60d3fa141d8c217164a85e3ae86b75aa317210bdd1ff5bd8cdfbc7.jpg)

In an overturned fold, one limb is pushed so far over that it tilts beyond the vertical, and both limbs dip in the same direction.

- This happens when compressional forces are very intense, causing extreme deformation of rock layers.

### RECUMBENT FOLD :

○ A recumbent fold is an extreme type of overturned fold where the axial plane is nearly horizontal, and the rock layers are folded so much that they lie almost flat on top of one another.

- Recumbent folds are common in high-grade metamorphic rocks formed deep within mountain belts.

![Geomorphic Processes – Figure 1](https://cdn-mineru.openxlab.org.cn/result/2026-09-02/d3afdc39-d85b-4dcd-bb42-06e7df48cde5/6aa69bea4e9ec7367522e57d36795f8028b981b22516435c2d54e1a1bc5f6416.jpg)

### ▶ OVERTHRUST FOLD :

As the compressional forces intensify, the recumbent fold stage evolves into an overthrust fold. In overthrust folds, the stress leads to the development of a thrust fault at a low angle, along which rock layers are displaced horizontally over one another.

The overthrusting leads to even greater crustal shortening and thickening, a process critical in mountain building.

### NAPPE :

The final stage in this cycle is the development of nappe folds, which represent the most extreme deformation caused by compressional forces. A nappe forms when large sheets of rock are thrust horizontally over vast distances.

- Nappe is the result of overthrusting becoming so intense that entire blocks of rock slide along a thrust plane, often completely detaching from their original positions.

- Nappes are often stacked on top of one another, contributing to the complexity and thickness of mountain ranges.

### ISOCLINAL FOLD :

○ An isoclinal fold is a type of fold in which both limbs are parallel to each other and the axial plane.

These folds result from very strong compressional forces, leading to a tight fold with nearly identical limbs.

### CHEVRON FOLD :

○ A chevron fold has sharp, angular hinges and straight limbs, forming a zigzag or V-shaped pattern.

These folds are typically symmetrical, with tight, pointed hinges, and result from brittle deformation under compressional forces. Chevron folds often form in rocks that are layered in alternating hard and soft materials, which respond differently to stress.

### NORMAL FAULT

A normal fault occurs when the crust is stretched and extended, causing one block of rock to move downward relative to the other. Though not as common in traditional mountain ranges, normal faults are associated with divergent boundaries or regions of crustal stretching, often found in fault-block mountains.

While normal faults are primarily associated with extensional tectonics, they can play a significant role in mountain building through the following processes:

### 1. FAULT - BLOCK MOUNTAINS :

- Normal faults can lead to the formation of fault-block mountains, which are a result of large crustal blocks being uplifted along fault planes. When the hanging wall drops along a normal fault, the footwall block is often uplifted, creating steep, rugged mountain ranges.

- Example : The Sierra Nevada Mountains in California are a classic example of fault-block mountains formed by normal faulting. The mountain range was uplifted as a large crustal block along a system of normal faults, while adjacent regions sank to form valleys.

![Geomorphic Processes – Figure 1](https://cdn-mineru.openxlab.org.cn/result/2026-09-02/d3afdc39-d85b-4dcd-bb42-06e7df48cde5/93d3e24128e10b968131503849d0957fef6f2da6bd0197ddee3035c8402ea83a.jpg)

### 2. HORST - GRABEN STRUCTURES :

- Normal faults are closely linked to the formation of rift valleys and horst-graben structures, which occur when the crust is stretched and broken into blocks. These

faulted blocks either drop down to form graben (depressed blocks) or rise to form horsts (uplifted blocks).

- Example : The East African Rift Valley is a prominent example where normal faulting has created a series of grabens and horsts, contributing to the development of highlands and mountain ranges like the Ethiopian Highlands.

### 3. CONTINENTAL RIFTING :

- Normal faulting is also associated with the early stages of continental rifting, where tectonic plates begin to break apart. Over time, this extension can result in the formation of new ocean basins and passive continental margins.

- Example : The Red Sea Rift is an active continental rift where normal faulting is causing the Arabian Plate to move away from the African Plate, eventually forming a new ocean basin.

## Mountains Are One Of Earth'S Most Striking Landforms, Formed Through Various Geological Processes. Based On Their Formation Mechanisms, Mountains Can Be Classified Into Several Types: Fold Mountains, Block Mountains, Volcanic Mountains And Residual Mountains.

### FOLD MOUNTAINS

Fold mountains are formed due to the compression of the Earth's crust, which results in the bending or folding of rock layers. They are created at convergent plate boundaries where two tectonic plates collide, causing horizontal compression that folds and uplifts the rock layers.

### FEATURES :

- Fold mountains are characterized by anticlines (upward folds) and synclines (downward folds).

- They are typically long, linear mountain chains with sharp and high peaks.

- Young fold mountains are still rising (e.g. Himalayas), while old fold mountains are more eroded (e.g. Aravalli Range)

### EXAMPLES :

- Himalayas (India-Nepal), Alps (Europe), Rocky Mountains (USA), Andes (South America)

Block mountains are formed due to the faulting and fracturing of the Earth's crust, which results in large blocks of land being uplifted or tilted. These mountains form through tensional forces at divergent plate boundaries or along large faults. The Earth's crust is stretched, causing large blocks to either rise (horst) or sink (graben).

### FEATURES :

- Block mountains have steep, rugged slopes with flat or slightly tilted summits.

- Horsts are the uplifted blocks and grabens are the sunken blocks, often forming valleys.

- The landscape is characterized by alternating elevated and depressed regions.

### EXAMPLES :

- Sierra Nevada (USA), Vosges (France), Black Forest (Germany), Satpura Range (India)

### VOLCANIC MOUNTAINS

Volcanic mountains are formed by the accumulation of lava, ash and other volcanic materials from volcanic eruptions. They are created when magma from beneath the Earth's crust erupts through the surface, solidifying into volcanic cones over time. Successive eruptions build up the mountain.

![Geomorphic Processes – Figure 1](https://cdn-mineru.openxlab.org.cn/result/2026-09-02/d3afdc39-d85b-4dcd-bb42-06e7df48cde5/61aa0a9d13f96da29f0f6b5fbceeeffc8ed31bc24561a3b7a47a3af36defc77e.jpg)

### FEATURES :

- They are typically cone-shaped with steep sides, a crater at the summit and often consist of multiple layers of hardened lava, volcanic ash and pumice.

- They can be classified as Active, dormant or extinct, depending on volcanic activity.

### EXAMPLES :

- Mount Fuji (Japan), Mount Kilimanjaro (Tanzania), Mount St. Helens (USA), Mount Vesuvius (Italy)

### RESIDUAL MOUNTAINS

Residual mountains are formed by the erosion of surrounding land, leaving behind harder, more resistant rock structures that stand as isolated mountains. These mountains are the remnants of older mountains that have been worn down by wind, water and other erosion processes over time, leaving behind more resistant rock formations.

### FEATURES :

- Irregular, isolated peaks or ridges, often surrounded by plains.

- Made up of hard rock that is more resistant to erosion compared to the softer surrounding material.

### EXAMPLES :

- Aravallis (India), Urals (Russia), Drakensberg Mountains (South Africa)

## Epeirogenic Processes

Epeirogenic movements are broad, slow and gentle vertical movements of the Earth's crust caused by endogenic geomorphic processes. Unlike orogenic movements that lead to mountain building, epeirogenic movements cause continental uplift or subsidence without much deformation. These movements occur due to isostatic adjustments in response to changes in the Earth's lithosphere and mantle, such as sediment deposition, glaciation or tectonic forces. They play a key role in shaping large-scale landforms like plateaus, plains and basins. As the Earth's surface rises or subsides, erosion and weathering act to further modify these features, leading to the formation of distinct landforms over time.

### TYPES OF PLATEAUS

Plateaus are elevated flat-topped landforms that rise sharply above the surrounding terrain and are formed through various geological processes. Based on their formation mechanisms, plateaus can be classified into several types: Piedmont plateau, Intermontane plateaus, Volcanic plateaus, Dissected plateaus and Continental plateaus.

A piedmont plateau is located at the base of a mountain or mountain range, formed between the mountains and nearby plains. Formed by the deposition of eroded materials (sediments) from nearby mountains or by tectonic uplift along the foot of a mountain range.

### FEATURES :

- Gently sloping towards the plains and higher towards the mountains.

- Rich in mineral deposits and fertile soils, often used for agriculture.

- Piedmont plateaus may experience continuous deposition of materials due to erosion from nearby mountains.

### EXAMPLES :

- Patagonia Plateau (South America), Appalachian Plateau (North America), Malwa Plateau (India)

### INTERMONTANE PLATEAU

An intermontane plateau is a highland region that lies between two or more mountain ranges. Formed by the uplift of the Earth's crust due to tectonic activity, often found in areas of intense orogenic (mountain-building) activity.

### FEATURES :

- Surrounded by high mountain ranges on all sides, making it one of the highest plateaus on Earth.

- Can have arid or semi-arid climates, with limited vegetation due to their altitude.

- Features broad, flat surfaces and may contain large valleys, basins or dry river beds.

### EXAMPLES :

- Bolivian Plateau (South America), Tibetan Plateau (by collision of Indian and European tectonic plates)

### VOLCANIC PLATEAU

A volcanic plateau is a large, elevated flat landform that is formed due to repeated volcanic activity over a long period of time. The eruption of lava and its subsequent spread over vast areas creates broad, flat surfaces that rise above the surrounding land.

### FORMATION :

- Volcanic plateaus are created by the outpouring of lava from cracks or fissures in the Earth's surface during volcanic eruptions.

- Over millions of years, successive layers of basaltic lava cool and harden, building up large, flat expanses.

- These plateaus are typically associated with shield volcanoes or fissure eruptions, where lava flows out smoothly and covers large areas rather than creating steep volcanic cones.

### FEATURES :

- Flat and extensive surfaces : Volcanic plateaus have gently sloping terrain due to the widespread flow of fluid lava.

- Composed of basaltic rocks : These plateaus are primarily formed of basalt, which is a dense, dark volcanic rock rich in iron and magnesium.

- Highly fertile soils : Due to the mineral-rich lava , soils on volcanic plateaus are often very fertile , supporting agriculture .

### EXAMPLES :

- Deccan Plateau (India), Columbia Plateau (USA)

### DISSECTED PLATEAU

Dissected plateaus represent an advanced stage of plateau evolution, where the action of natural agents like [[Rivers]] and streams has carved the plateau into intricate landscapes. These landforms are often found in regions that were originally uplifted by tectonic forces, but where erosion has since played a significant role in shaping the topography.

### FORMATION :

The formation of a dissected plateau typically begins with the uplift of a landmass, which could occur due to tectonic forces such as the collision of continental plates or the thermal expansion of the Earth's crust. Once the plateau is uplifted, it becomes subject to erosional forces over millions of years.

### FEATURES :

- Steep Slopes and Escarpments : Dissected plateaus feature sharp ridges, cliffs and steep slopes where [[Rivers]] have cut through the elevated surface.

- Deep Valleys and Gorges : Erosion by [[Rivers]] creates deep, narrow valleys and sometimes dramatic gorges.

- Elevated Ridges : Parts of the plateau that have not been eroded often remain as high ridges or hills.

- Complex Drainage Systems : [[Rivers]] and streams in dissected plateaus typically form dendritic (tree-like) drainage patterns due to the rugged terrain.

- Colorado Plateau (USA) : The Colorado Plateau, located in the western United States, is a classic example of a dissected plateau. The erosion by the Colorado River has created dramatic landforms like the Grand Canyon, where the plateau has been deeply incised over millions of years.

### CONTINENTAL PLATEAU

A continental plateau is a large, elevated flat landmass that rises sharply above the surrounding area, forming on continental crust. These plateaus are typically vast, and they represent the uplift of an entire region due to tectonic processes. Unlike volcanic or intermontane plateaus, continental plateaus are not closely associated with volcanic activity or mountain ranges but are usually uplifted as part of large, stable continental interiors.

### FEATURES :

- Flat or Gently Undulating Surface : Continental plateaus generally have broad, flat or gently rolling surfaces, which contrast with the steep, rugged topography of fold mountains.

- High Elevation : These plateaus are typically located at high elevations, often rising several thousand meters above sea level.

- Steep Margins : Continental plateaus usually have steep sides where they meet lower-lying regions. This creates sharp escarpments or cliffs, distinguishing them from the surrounding landscape.

- Stable Crust : These plateaus are often located in the stable parts of the continental crust, with little active seismic or volcanic activity. They are typically found away from tectonic boundaries.

### EXAMPLES :

- The plateaus of western Australia and Africa are examples of Continental Plateau.

- ‘The Plateau of Maharashtra’ is an example of the continental plateau in India.

## Exogenic Forces Are The External Processes That Operate On The Earth'S Surface And Are Primarily Responsible For Shaping And Modifying The Landscape. These Forces Originate From Outside The Earth'S Surface And Are Driven By Various Agents Like Water, Wind, Ice And Waves. Unlike Endogenic Forces, Which Are Internal And Responsible For Creating Landforms (Such As Through Tectonic And Volcanic Activity), Exogenic Forces Work To Break Down, Transport And Deposit Materials, Leading To The Gradual Degradation Of Landforms.

These forces are also known as denudational forces because they wear down the Earth's surface through a combination of weathering, mass wasting, erosion and deposition. The energy for exogenic processes comes from the Sun's heat and the gravitational forces acting on the Earth's surface.

### WEATHERING

Weathering refers to the breakdown of rocks into smaller particles through physical, chemical, and biological processes. It weakens rocks and makes them more susceptible to erosion. Weathering processes are conditioned by many complex geological, climate, topographic and vegetative factors. The following are the three major weathering processes:

1. PHYSICAL / MECHANICAL WEATHERING : refers to the process of breaking down rocks and minerals into smaller pieces without altering their chemical composition. This type of weathering is primarily driven by physical forces, which could be :

- Gravitational forces such as overburden pressure, load and shearing stress

- Expansion forces due to temperature changes

• Water pressure controlled by wetting and drying cycles

The following are the key physical weathering processes :

EXFOLIATION : occurs when overlying layers of rock are eroded away, reducing the pressure on the underlying rock. This release of pressure allows the rock to expand, causing layers or sheets to peel off.

➢ THERMAL EXPANSION : occurs when rocks are subjected to rapid heating and cooling, particularly in desert environments. As the rock heats up during the day, it expands, and

when it cools at night, it contracts. Over time, these repeated cycles of expansion and contraction cause the outer layers of rock to crack and flake off. This process is most effective in arid regions with extreme temperature variations between day and night. It results in the formation of smaller rock fragments.

➢ ABRASION : occurs when rocks are worn down by friction and collision with other rock particles. This can happen in [[Rivers]], glaciers, deserts or coastal environments where wind, water or ice carries particles that grind against rocks, smoothing and rounding them.

➢ FROST WEDGING ( FREEZE - THAW CYCLE ) : occurs due to growth of ice within pores and cracks of rocks during repeated cycles of freezing and melting . Rapid freezing of water causes its sudden expansion and high pressure . The resulting expansion affects joints, cracks and small inter - granular fractures to become wider till the rock breaks apart .

> SALT WEATHERING : Salt crystal growth occurs when salty water enters the cracks of rocks. As the water evaporates, salt crystals are left behind, which can grow and expand, exerting pressure on the rock.

- Salts in rocks expand due to thermal action hydration and crystallisation. Many salts like calcium, sodium, magnesium, potassium and barium have a tendency to expand, with expansion varying according to temperature and their thermal properties.

2. CHEMICAL WEATHERING : involves the alteration of the chemical structure of minerals in rocks, mainly through reactions with water, air and acids.

- A group of weathering processes viz solution, carbonation, hydration, oxidation and reduction act on the rocks to decompose, dissolve or reduce them to a clastic state through chemical reactions by oxygen, surface and/or soil water and other acids.

- Water and air ( oxygen and carbon dioxide ) along with heat must be present to speed up all chemical reactions .

The following are the key chemical weathering processes :

> SOLUTION: when something is dissolved in water or acids, then the dissolved content is called 'Solution'.

In the solution process, minerals dissolve directly into water without the need for chemical alteration. Water, particularly if slightly acidic (due to dissolved carbon dioxide), dissolves minerals like halite (NaCl), gypsum
(\mathrm{CaSO}_4)
and calcite
(\mathrm{CaCO}_3)
. The minerals are removed from the rock and carried away in solution.

Solution weathering is most effective in regions with high rainfall and where water moves freely through rocks. It is responsible for the formation of karst topography, including features like limestone caves.

CARBONATION : is the reaction of carbonate and bicarbonate with minerals and is a common process helping the breaking down of feldspars and carbonate minerals.

It occurs when carbon dioxide
(\mathrm{CO}_{2})
from the atmosphere or soil dissolves in water to form carbonic acid
(\mathrm{H}_{2}\mathrm{CO}_{3})
, which reacts with minerals, especially those rich in calcium carbonate (such as limestone). This acid dissolves the carbonate minerals, leading to the breakdown of the rock.

HYDRATION : is the chemical addition of water. Minerals take up water and expand, which causes an increase in the volume of the material itself or rock.

> OXIDATION : means a combination of a mineral with oxygen to form oxides or hydroxides. In the process of oxidation, rock breakdown occurs due to the disturbance caused by addition of oxygen.

This process primarily affects iron-bearing minerals (like olivine, pyroxene, and amphibole), leading to the formation of oxides or hydroxides. As iron reacts with oxygen, it forms iron oxides (rust), which weakens the rock and changes its color. Red color of iron upon oxidation turns to brown or yellow.

> REDUCTION : when oxidised minerals are placed in an environment where oxygen is absent, reduction takes place. Such conditions exist usually below the water table, in areas of stagnant water and waterlogged ground.

- Red color of iron upon reduction turns to a greenish or bluish color.

3. BIOLOGICAL WEATHERING : is the weakening and subsequent disintegration of rock by plants, animals and microbes . It works in combination with physical and chemical weathering processes but is distinct because organisms are directly involved . It can occur due to physical stress like in case of penetration of plant's roots , physical impact of the hooves of the animal etc. or due to chemical changes caused by them like action of worms , lichen etc .

The following are the few biological weathering processes :

- Burrowing and wedging by organisms like earthworms, termites etc help in exposing the new surfaces to chemical attack and assists in the penetration of moisture and air.

- Ploughing and cultivating soil also help in mixing and creating new contacts between air, water and minerals in the earth materials.

- Decaying plant and animal matter helps in the production of humic, carbonic and other acids which enhance decay and solubility of some elements.

EROSION : involves the removal of weathered material by agents like water, wind, glaciers and sea waves. The eroded material is transported from one place to another.

TRANSPORTATION : The eroded materials are transported by the agents of erosion such as [[Rivers]], glaciers, wind and ocean currents. The transportation distance and sorting of material depend on the energy of the transporting agent.

DEPOSITION : occurs when the transporting agent loses its energy and deposits the eroded material. This can lead to the formation of various landforms like floodplains, deltas, sand dunes and moraines.

Mass movement, also known as mass wasting, refers to the downhill movement of rock, soil and debris under the direct influence of gravity. Mass movement is primarily driven by gravity but is influenced by factors such as weathering, slope steepness, water saturation, vegetation and human activities.

Following are the factors affecting the mass movement :

- Slope Angle : The steeper the slope, the greater the potential for mass movement

- Water Content : Water acts as a lubricant, increasing the likelihood of material sliding downhill. Saturated soils are more prone to movement.

- Vegetation : Roots help to bind soil particles and stabilize slopes. The absence of vegetation, due to deforestation or natural events, increases mass movement risk.

- Human Activities : Construction, mining and deforestation triggers the removal of support from below to the materials above, disturbing natural slopes and triggers mass movements.

- Natural Causes : occurrence of earthquakes, explosion or machinery causes mass movement

The mass movements can be grouped under two major classes :

- Slow Movements

- Rapid Movements

![Geomorphic Processes – Figure 1](https://cdn-mineru.openxlab.org.cn/result/2026-09-02/d3afdc39-d85b-4dcd-bb42-06e7df48cde5/1100e62adfb6057ddc89a0682dcd53c906ead80eafc8fe2c18c66b341c427555.jpg)

Slow movement refers to the gradual, almost imperceptible downslope movement of soil, rock and debris. These movements are spread over long periods and often go unnoticed until significant changes have occurred. The slow movements include types such as:

- SOIL CREEP : The slow, steady downhill movement of soil and loose rock particles due to gravity. It is most common in areas with a moderate slope and affects the surface layers. Soil expands during wet conditions or freezing temperatures and contracts during dry conditions or thawing. This cycle of expansion and contraction gradually moves soil particles down slope.

- ROCK CREEP : is similar to soil creep , rock creep is the slow movement of weathered rock material down a slope . Rocks that have been fragmented by weathering move very slowly due to gravity , usually in regions with fractured rock layers .

- SOLIFLUCTION : A type of slow movement found in cold , periglacial regions where the top layer of soil thaws and flows over the frozen subsoil (permafrost) . During the summer , the top layer of soil becomes saturated with water and starts moving slowly downhill .

### RAPID MOVEMENTS

Rapid mass movements are sudden, fast and often catastrophic events. These movements occur at a much faster pace than slow movements and can cause significant destruction. The types of rapid movements are:

- ROCKFALL : is the free fall of individual rocks or rock fragments from a steep slope or cliff. Weathering (e.g. frost action) or seismic activity weakens rocks at high elevations, causing them to detach and fall freely due to gravity. Rock fragments collect at the base of the slope, forming a talus slope.

- DEBRIS FLOW : is a rapid flow of water - saturated debris , including soil , rock and organic material , down a slope . Triggered by intense rainfall or sudden snowmelt , debris flows move rapidly and behave like a fluid , carrying large amounts of loose material .

- MUD FLOW : A type of debris flow where the material is primarily composed of fine particles like silt and clay, mixed with water. They are common after heavy rainfall in areas with little vegetation and in volcanic regions after eruptions.

- LANDSLIDE : is the rapid sliding of a mass of rock , debris or earth down a slope . Landslides occur when the gravitational force acting on a slope overcomes the internal strength of the material, causing it to slide . Landslides are often triggered by heavy rainfall, earthquakes , volcanic eruptions or human activities like deforestation and mining . Two types :

- Rotational Slide (Slump) : The mass of material moves downwards along a concave surface, causing a backward rotation of the sliding mass.

○ Translational Slide : The movement occurs along a more or less flat surface, often leading to a large-scale, rapid movement of material.

These Landforms Are Shaped By Exogenic Forces, Which Are External Processes Such As Erosion, Transportation And Deposition Driven By Water Flow. [[Rivers]], Being A Major Agent Of These Forces, Carve, Modify And Build Up Landscapes Over Time Through The Movement Of Sediment And Water.

Key Characteristics of Fluvial Landforms :

- Erosion : [[Rivers]] erode land by cutting into their beds and banks, primarily through mechanical action (such as abrasion and hydraulic action) and chemical weathering (like solution).

- Transportation : [[Rivers]] transport eroded material downstream through various processes like suspension, traction, saltation and solution.

- Deposition : When a river loses energy, it deposits the materials it carries, forming features like deltas, floodplains and alluvial fans.

### NOTE :

- TRACTION EROSION : is a geological process where large rocks are rolled or dragged along the bottom of a river or shore by the force of the current

These processes result in a variety of landforms, both erosional and depositional, which reflect the energy, velocity and sediment load of the river at different stages of its course.

### V - SHAPED VALLEY

- V - shaped valley is a narrow, steep - sided valley with a sharp profile, typical of the youthful stage of a river's development. The river erodes vertically, cutting down into the bedrock.

- For example : The Indus River Valley in the Himalayas and the valleys of many mountain streams

- For example : The Narmada Gorge in central India

### CANYONS

- A canyon is a larger version of a gorge, typically found in arid or semi-arid regions. It is formed through prolonged vertical erosion by a river.

### CATARACTS

- A cataract is a large waterfall, characterized by a significant drop in elevation over a relatively short distance. Cataracts are often formed where a river flows over resistant rock layers, leading to a dramatic and powerful flow of water.

- Cataracts are distinguished by their substantial vertical drop, which can vary from several meters to over a hundred meters.

### PLUNGE POOL

- A plunge pool is a deep basin or depression formed at the base of a waterfall or a cataract, created by the erosional forces of falling water. As the water cascades down from a height, it impacts the riverbed, eroding the underlying material and leading to the development of a plunge pool.

- Plunge pools often have a round or oval shape, reflecting the circular motion of water as it impacts and swirls within the pool.

### RAPIDS

- Rapids are sections of a river where the water flows quickly over a steep or uneven riverbed, resulting in turbulent and fast-moving water. They can occur in various forms, from short stretches of rapid flow to longer, continuous segments.

### MEANDERS (EROSIONAL + DEPOSITIONAL)

- Meanders are sweeping, sinuous bends in a river. They form in the middle and lower courses of a river, where the gradient is gentle, and the river's flow begins to erode laterally rather than vertically.

- Meanders form through a process of lateral erosion on the outer banks (due to the high velocity) and deposition on the inner banks (due to the lower velocity). Over time, this creates looping curves.

- For example : Meanders along the Mississippi River in the USA

### FEATURES :

1. Cut Bank (Erosional feature) : The cut bank is the outer, steeper side of a meander where erosion occurs due to the faster flow of water

2. Point Bar (Depositional feature): The point bar is the inner side of a meander, where sediment is deposited due to the slower flow

3. Oxbow Lake : An oxbow lake is a crescent - shaped water body that forms when a meander is cut off from the main river channel due to the river's lateral erosion . ### DEPOSITIONAL

Fluvial Depositional Landforms Are Created By The Deposition Of Sediments Carried By [[Rivers]] And Streams As Their Energy Decreases. As The River Slows Down, It Loses Its Capacity To Transport Materials And Deposits Them Along Its Course, Forming Distinct Landforms. These Features Are Mainly Found In The Middle And Lower Courses Of [[Rivers]], Where Sedimentation Becomes More Pronounced Due To Reduced Gradient And Velocity.

### ALLUVIAL FANS

An alluvial fan is a cone - shaped deposit of sediment formed where a river flows out of a steep mountain range onto a flat plain.

- As the river exits a narrow valley and enters a flat plain, its velocity decreases rapidly, causing the sediment it carries to spread out and deposit in a fan shape. Coarse materials like gravel and pebbles are deposited first, followed by finer sediments further away.

- They are composed of poorly sorted sediments and have a characteristic fan - like shape.

Levees are natural embankments formed alongside [[Rivers]] as a result of sediment deposition during floods.

- Levees help in confining the flow of [[Rivers]], sometimes preventing them from spilling over into the floodplain during minor floods. They may be breached during severe floods, leading to more catastrophic flooding.

A floodplain is the flat, wide area of land along either side of a river that is subject to periodic flooding.

- They are formed as the river overflows its banks during floods, depositing fine sediments like silt and clay across the valley floor. Over time, the accumulation of these deposits leads to the creation of a fertile plain, ideal for agriculture.

- Floodplains are composed of layers of alluvium, deposited by the river during successive floods. They tend to have rich, fertile soil due to the regular deposition of nutrient-rich sediment.

### DELTA

A delta is a landform that forms at the mouth of a river, where it meets a standing body of water such as a sea or lake, and deposits its sediment load.

- When the river enters the still water, its velocity reduces drastically, causing the river to deposit sediments. Over time, these deposits build up to form a delta, with branching distributaries.

- Deltas are made up of fine sediment like silt and clay, and are usually rich in nutrients, making them highly fertile. Deltas take various shapes such as arcuate (fan-shaped), bird's foot and cuspate.

- For example : The Nile Delta in Egypt, known for its triangular shape

River drainage patterns refer to the arrangement of a river system and its tributaries within a given landscape. These patterns are shaped by various factors, including the geological features of the terrain, the slope of the land, the climate and the underlying rock types. The primary types of river drainage patterns include dendritic, radial, rectangular, trellis and parallel.

### DENDRITIC DRAINAGE PATTERN

- The dendritic pattern resembles a tree or branching structure, with a main river and several tributaries that branch off at various angles.

- This pattern develops in areas with relatively uniform rock types and gentle slopes, where the river meanders freely and follows the path of least resistance.

- Many [[Rivers]] in sedimentary rock regions, such as the Mississippi River, Indus River, Ganga River etc exhibit this pattern.

### TRELLIS DRAINAGE PATTERN

- The trellis pattern resembles a garden trellis, with a main river running parallel to the mountain ranges, while tributaries join it at right angles.

- This pattern forms in regions of alternating hard and soft rock layers, where streams erode the softer rock while the harder rock resists erosion, leading to a trellis-like arrangement.

- The Appalachian Mountains exhibit a trellis drainage pattern. Another is the river patterns in Chota Nagpur Plateau.

### RECTANGULAR DRAINAGE PATTERN

- This pattern features a series of right-angle bends and straight segments, resembling a rectangular grid.

- The rectangular pattern develops in regions with a faulted or jointed bedrock, where the streams follow the fractures in the rock.

- The Colorado River and the streams in the Vindhyan Mountain systems show characteristics of a rectangular drainage pattern.

RECTANGULAR DRAINAGE PATTERN

### CENTRIPETAL DRAINAGE PATTERN

- A centripetal drainage pattern is characterized by [[Rivers]] and streams that flow inward toward a central point, forming a network that resembles a funnel or a basin.

- Centripetal drainage typically occurs in depressions or low-lying areas, such as basins, lakes, or crater-like formations.

- As water from surrounding areas drains inward, it accumulates in the central basin, leading to the formation of lakes or wetland areas.

- The Kathmandu Valley in Nepal and the Raigarh Dome in the Lower Chambal Basin of India are examples of regions with centripetal drainage patterns

### SEA-WAVE LANDFORMS

**_Sea-Wave Landforms Are Created By The Continuous Action Of Waves, Tides And Ocean Currents Along Coastlines. These Exogenic Forces Erode, Transport And Deposit Materials, Shaping A Variety Of Erosional And Depositional Landforms. The Energy Generated By Sea Waves, Driven Primarily By Wind, Interacts With Coastal Rocks And Sediments, Leading To The Formation Of Unique Features Over Time.**


### EROSIONAL

The constant action of sea waves along coastlines plays a major role in shaping various erosional landforms. The energy carried by waves, combined with the abrasive effect of sand, pebbles and other sediments, causes the breakdown of coastal rocks and cliffs. Over time, this results in distinctive landforms.

![Geomorphic Processes – Figure 1](https://cdn-mineru.openxlab.org.cn/result/2026-09-02/d3afdc39-d85b-4dcd-bb42-06e7df48cde5/c110c133de61e1e51f96417a98e5cb3ec35b486fb2cf93fcea998c84e7e6a1b2.jpg)

### HEADLAND & BAY

- Headlands are protruding landforms of resistant rock, while bays are recessed areas of softer rock that are eroded more quickly.

- Differential erosion is key to the formation of headlands and bays. In coastal areas with alternating layers of hard and soft rock, the softer rock is eroded faster, creating bays, while the harder rock remains, forming headlands.

![Geomorphic Processes – Figure 1](https://cdn-mineru.openxlab.org.cn/result/2026-09-02/d3afdc39-d85b-4dcd-bb42-06e7df48cde5/cff181eb33300d8f0c649d0f1b0fb02cf2750e25879628c67702ff93bfb7cf44.jpg)

### SEA - CLIFF

- A cliff is a steep, near-vertical rock face formed by the continuous erosion of the coastline.

- Cliffs are created when waves hit the base of the coastline, undercutting it through processes like hydraulic action and abrasion. As the base of the rock erodes, the overlying material becomes unstable and collapses, causing the cliff to retreat inland over time.

### NOTCH

- A notch is a shallow, horizontal indentation or groove cut into the base of a coastal cliff or rock face by the erosive action of sea waves.

- Notches are an early stage in the formation of larger features like sea caves or wave-cut platforms.

### SEA CAVE

- They are the hollow cavities or tunnels formed in coastal cliffs due to the erosive action of waves.

- Waves exploit weaknesses in the rock, such as cracks, joints or bedding planes. The repeated impact of water, particularly during storms, erodes the rock, creating a cave. Sea caves are typically found at the base of cliffs.

### SEA ARCH

- A natural arch formed when waves erode through a headland, creating an opening.

- The erosion process begins with the formation of sea caves on opposite sides of a headland. Over time, continued erosion causes the caves to enlarge and meet, forming an arch that extends from one side of the headland to the other.

### SEA STACK / CHIMNEY ROCK / SKERRIES

- Stacks are isolated, vertical columns of rock that stand offshore, separated from the main coastline.

- Stacks are formed when an arch collapses. The roof of the arch eventually becomes too weak to support its own weight, and it falls, leaving behind a column of rock (the stack) that was previously part of the headland.

### DEPOSITIONAL

While sea waves erode coastal areas, they also transport and deposit sediments such as sand, gravel and pebbles, forming various depositional landforms. These features arise where wave energy is lower or where sediments carried by waves are deposited in calm coastal environments. Depositional processes shape coastlines, creating beaches, spits and other landforms that evolve over time.

### BEACHES

- A beach is a gently sloping accumulation of sand, gravel or pebbles along the shoreline, deposited by waves.

- Beaches form when waves transport sediment from offshore and deposit it along the coast. Waves with less energy deposit finer materials, while more energetic waves deposit coarser materials.

### SAND SPIT

- A spit is a projected deposition extending from the coast into the sea, often with a hooked end.

- Spits are formed by longshore drift, a process where waves approach the shore at an angle, moving sediments along the coast. When the coastline changes direction or there is a decrease in wave energy, the sediment is deposited, forming a spit. The hooked end can form due to changes in wind direction or wave energy.

### SAND BAR

- A bar is a ridge of sand that forms parallel to the coast, either partially or completely enclosing a body of water, such as a lagoon.

- Bars form when sediments are transported offshore by waves and currents and then deposited in relatively shallow waters. Bars can form just offshore, or they can connect two headlands or enclose a lagoon.

### BAY BARRIER

- Barrier islands are long, narrow islands of sand or sediment that run parallel to the mainland coast, separated by a lagoon or a bay.

- Barrier islands provide a natural defense against coastal erosion and storm surges by absorbing wave energy.

![Geomorphic Processes – Figure 1](https://cdn-mineru.openxlab.org.cn/result/2026-09-02/d3afdc39-d85b-4dcd-bb42-06e7df48cde5/aeea7ca5306265ba1a33626e71546420148a6f1ac42fd484967f867339931063.jpg)

### LAGOON LAKE

- A lagoon is a shallow body of water separated from the sea by a sandbar, barrier island or reef.

- Lagoons form when a bar, spit or barrier island encloses a portion of the sea, creating a body of water that is protected from direct wave action.

### TOMBOLO

- A tombolo is a narrow strip of sand that connects an island to the mainland or another island.

- It is formed by the deposition of sediments due to wave action, specifically through wave refraction and longshore drift. As waves bend around an island, their energy decreases on the leeward side, allowing sediments to accumulate in a ridge, eventually creating the tombolo.

Ria, Fjord and Dalmatian coastlines are unique coastal landforms created by the interaction of tectonic activity, glaciation and sea-level changes. ### RIA COASTLINE

- A Ria coastline is characterized by a series of drowned river valleys that have been submerged by rising sea levels.

# Wind
- formed landforms are a key feature of arid and semi - arid regions , where exogenic forces such as wind (also called aeolian forces) play a dominant role in shaping the landscape. Wind is a powerful agent of erosion , transportation and deposition in areas with little vegetation cover, where loose particles such as sand , silt and dust can be easily moved .

### EROSIONAL

Erosional landforms created by wind , particularly in arid and semi-arid regions , are shaped by the process of wind erosion or aeolian erosion . In areas with little vegetation , high temperatures and scarce rainfall , wind becomes a dominant agent of erosion , capable of shaping the landscape over time through the processes of deflation , abrasion and attrition . These wind-driven forces gradually carve out distinctive features , especially in desert regions where loose sand and other particles are easily transported .

### NOTE :

- *DEFLATION* : Wind removes and carries away loose, fine-grained particles of dust or sediment

• ABRASION : Wind-borne sand grains grind against surfaces and wearing them down

- ATTRITION : Sand particles wear down and break apart as they collide with each other while being transported by the wind

### ROCK PEDESTAL ( MUSHROOM ROCK )

- Rock pedestals, also known as mushroom rocks, are isolated rock formations that have a narrow base and a wider top, resembling a mushroom. They are formed by wind erosion at different heights along the rock surface.

- Wind erosion is most effective near the ground, where sand is blown against the base of the rock. Over time, the lower part of the rock is eroded more quickly than the upper part, forming a narrow base and a wider top.

- Demoiselles are tall, thin columns of rock that are left standing as the softer rock around them erodes away. These landforms are often capped by a harder, more resistant rock layer that protects the column from rapid erosion.

- Their height varies depending on the thickness of the rock layers and the rate of erosion.

### YARDANG

- Yardangs are streamlined, elongated ridges carved into the desert surface by wind erosion. They typically form parallel to the prevailing wind direction.

- Yardangs form when strong, consistent winds erode softer, less resistant rock layers, leaving behind the harder, more resistant layers as ridges. Wind abrasion further shapes these features into aerodynamic forms.

### ZEUGEN

- Zeugen are tabular masses or ridges of rock formed when harder rock layers are left standing as softer rock layers are eroded away by wind.

- Zeugen often have a flat, hard top surface and a softer, eroded base, giving them a layered appearance. Wind erosion works on alternating layers of hard and soft rock. The softer layers are eroded more quickly, while the harder layers remain, forming tabular features.

### INSELBERG

- Inselbergs are isolated hills or mountains that rise abruptly from flat desert plains. They are remnants of ancient landforms that have resisted wind erosion while the surrounding softer materials were eroded away.

- Inselbergs are usually steep - sided and can be very tall compared to the surrounding landscape.

### MESAS & BUTTES

- Both mesas and buttes form as a result of differential erosion, a process where softer rock layers erode faster than harder, more resistant layers.

- The horizontal strata of harder rock protect the underlying softer layers from rapid erosion, leading to the creation of steep cliffs and flat tops.

### - MESA :

○ A mesa is a large, flat-topped hill with steep sides. The word "mesa" comes from the Spanish word for "table", due to its table - like appearance.

○ Mesas are typically wider than they are tall, with an expansive flat top and steep cliffs on all sides.

The top layer of a mesa is often made of a hard, erosion-resistant rock that protects the underlying softer rock layers from weathering.

### - BUTTE :

○ A butte is a smaller, more isolated landform with a similar structure to a mesa but with a much smaller surface area on top. Buttes are essentially smaller remnants of mesas.

- Buttes are formed when the top of a mesa continues to erode, leaving a smaller, isolated hill. They are mainly separated in arid regions by deep gorge or canyons.

### DREIKANTER

- Dreikanter are angular, wind - sculpted rocks with three flat faces ( hence the name "dreikanter" , which is German for "three-edged" ). These features are formed through the continuous erosion by wind-blown sand particles , a process known as wind abrasion .

- Dreikanters form due to the consistent , one - directional action of wind-blown sand grains striking the surface of a rock . As the sand particles hit the rock , they erode its surface, creating flat , smooth faces .

- As the wind direction changes slightly over time or due to shifting sand dunes, different sides of the rock are eroded, eventually producing three (or more) flat faces.

- As the rock is further eroded, it may be rotated or shifted by the wind or gravity, allowing more surfaces to be exposed to the wind. The rock's edges become increasingly sharp and polished, producing the typical angular shape of a dreikanter.

### LOESS

- Loess is fine-grained, windblown silt that is transported over long distances and deposited in thick layers. These deposits often originate from glacial outwash plains or deserts and are carried by strong winds.

- Loess deposits are typically very fertile and provide a good base for agriculture, as seen in regions like the Loess Plateau in China.

- The deposits are often deep and uniform in texture, forming extensive plains.

### BOLSON

- A bolson is a large, flat-bottomed desert valley or basin surrounded by mountains, typically found in arid regions.

- It is a closed basin where there is no external drainage, meaning water that enters either evaporates or infiltrates the ground.

### - PEDIMENT :

The bolson landforms are enclosed by mountain ranges, with alluvial fans often forming along the base of the mountains.

- They occur when ephemeral streams from the mountain lose energy and deposit sediments. The coarsest materials are deposited near the mountain, while finer materials settle further away.

### - BAJADA :

○ A bajada is a broad, gently sloping surface created by the merging of multiple alluvial fans along the base of a mountain range.

These fans coalesce to form a continuous apron of sediments, which accumulate through intermittent water flow during flash floods.

### - PLAYAS :

Due to evaporation, the center of a bolson may contain playas (dry lake beds) or salt flats where water temporarily collects and evaporates, leaving behind salts.

### KARST ( GROUNDWATER ) LANDFORMS

## Karst Topography Is A Distinctive Landscape Formed By The Dissolution Of Soluble Rocks, Such As Limestone, Dolomite And Gypsum, Through The Action Of Water. These Landforms Are Shaped Primarily By Chemical Weathering And Are Characterized By Underground Drainage Systems With Caves, Sinkholes And Other Features Resulting From The Interaction Between Water And Rock. Exogenic Forces, Particularly Erosion By Underground Water, Play A Crucial Role In Forming Karst Landscapes.

### EROSIONAL

## Karst Erosional Landforms Are Created By The Erosional Processes Of Underground Water, Primarily In Areas Where Soluble Rocks, Such As Limestone, Gypsum Or Dolomites. Karst Topography Results From The Dissolution Of These Rocks By Slightly Acidic Water (Often Containing Carbonic Acid Formed When Water Reacts With Carbon Dioxide). These Landforms Are Shaped By Exogenic Forces Acting Through The Movement Of Water Below The Earth'S Surface. The Unique Features Of Karst Landscapes Include Caves, Sinkholes And Underground [[Rivers]], Which Form Due To The Erosion And Dissolution Of Bedrock.

### KARST VALLEY

- Karst valleys, also known as blind valleys, are valleys that appear to terminate abruptly, with no surface river or stream exiting the valley.

- These valleys form when surface [[Rivers]] or streams are diverted underground into a karst drainage system, leaving the valley floor dry. Over time, the valley may become increasingly enclosed as the surrounding rock dissolves.

- Karst valleys often have steep sides and flat floors and they may contain sinkholes or swallow holes where surface water disappears underground, forming caves in the underground.

### SINKHOLES

- Sinkholes are funnel - shaped depressions or holes in the ground that form due to the dissolution of surface limestone. They are common in karst landscapes and vary greatly in size and depth.

### SHALLOW HOLES

- They develop in regions where soluble rock is present near the surface. Water, particularly from [[Rivers]] or streams, flows into the ground through these openings and continues its journey in underground [[Rivers]] and caves.

- Swallow holes often mark the entrance to underground cave systems.

### DOLINES

- The term ‘ doline ’ is sometimes used to refer to the collapse sinks . They are small bowl shaped enclosed depressions in the Karst terrain .

### UVALAS

- Uvalas are large, irregular depressions formed by the merging of several sinkholes, or sinkholes and dolines.

- These features typically form in areas where the dissolution of limestone occurs on a larger scale.

### POLJE

- Poljes form from the continued dissolution and subsidence of limestone over a broad area. They are often associated with underground [[Rivers]] that intermittently flood the depression, creating seasonal lakes.

- During periods of high rainfall, they may fill with water, creating temporary lakes. Poljes are important agricultural areas in some karst regions due to their fertile soils.

- Limestone pavements are flat, exposed areas of limestone bedrock that have been weathered into distinct block-like features, often separated by deep cracks known as grikes.

- As rainwater seeps into the joints and cracks in the limestone, it dissolves the rock, widening the fissures into deep, vertical clefts. The remaining blocks, called clints, are left standing between the grikes.

### DEPOSITIONAL

Depositional landforms created by karst underground water result from the process of precipitation of dissolved minerals. When water rich in calcium carbonate (CaCO₃) seeps through cracks and voids in the rock, it slowly deposits minerals as it drips or flows, forming distinct features such as stalactites, stalagmites, pillars and sometimes flowstones. These landforms occur in karst landscapes, where the dissolution and deposition of minerals over time shape the underground terrain.

### STALACTITES

- Stalactites are icicle-shaped formations that hang from the ceiling of a cave. They form as water rich in calcium carbonate drips slowly from the roof of a cave, leaving behind a tiny ring of calcite at the point where the water drop falls.

- As water drips from the ceiling, it loses
CO_{2}
and leaves behind a deposit of calcium carbonate. Over time, this process results in the downward growth of a stalactite.

- Stalactites are characterized by their elongated, pointed shape. They often grow very slowly, sometimes taking thousands of years to form just a few inches.

### STALAGMITES

- Stalagmites are upward-growing formations that rise from the floor of a cave, directly beneath a stalactite. They form from the accumulation of minerals dropped by water dripping from the cave ceiling.

- As water drips from a stalactite, it hits the cave floor and deposits calcium carbonate, leading to the slow upward growth of a stalagmite. Over time, the stalagmite grows taller as more minerals accumulate.

- Stalagmites are typically blunt or rounded at the top, unlike the pointed shape of stalactites.

![Geomorphic Processes – Figure 1](https://cdn-mineru.openxlab.org.cn/result/2026-09-02/d3afdc39-d85b-4dcd-bb42-06e7df48cde5/63e9c2cd88bdbb98bc82fe855bfb7281d643d6a08076deeefa48aa6730cc6964.jpg)

- Columns or pillars are formed when a stalactite and a stalagmite grow together and meet, creating a single continuous feature from the cave ceiling to the floor.

- Columns can vary in size and shape, depending on the growth rate of the stalactite and stalagmite. They are often among the most spectacular features in karst caves.

Masses of ice moving as sheets over the land or as linear flows down the slopes of mountains in broad troughs - like valleys are called glaciers. The movement of glaciers is slow unlike water flow. The movement could be a few centimetres to a few metres a day or even less or more.

## Glacial Landforms Are Created By The Movement And Activity Of Glaciers, Which Are Large Masses Of Ice That Form In Regions Where Snow Accumulation Exceeds Melting Over Long Periods. Glaciers Move Basically Because Of The Force Of Gravity And Act As Powerful Agents Of Erosion, Transportation And Deposition, Sculpting The Landscape Into Unique Features As They Move Across The Earth'S Surface.

### EROSIONAL

## Glacial Erosional Landforms Are Shaped By The Powerful Movement Of Glaciers As They Advance And Retreat, Acting As Agents Of Erosion Through Processes Like Plucking And Abrasion. Erosion By Glaciers Is Tremendous Because Of Friction Caused By Sheer Weight Of The Ice. The Material Plucked From The Land By Glaciers Gets Dragged Along The Floors Or Sides Of The Valleys And Causes Great Deformation Through Abrasion And Plucking.

### NOTE :

- PLUCKING : is the process of removing large chunks of rock from a glacier's surface. This happens when rocks freeze to the bottom of a glacier and are then carried away by the moving ice

- ABRASION : is defined as the erosion that occurs when particles scrape against each other

![Geomorphic Processes – Figure 1](https://cdn-mineru.openxlab.org.cn/result/2026-09-02/d3afdc39-d85b-4dcd-bb42-06e7df48cde5/d8cf8714d320452ec4b0e0e3bc7fd27796e9f4ecf0d6dac1bafcd731aaceafba.jpg)

- U - shaped valleys are broad, flat - bottomed valleys with steep, straight sides. They are one of the most distinctive features of glacial erosion.

- As glaciers move through pre-existing river valleys, they widen, deepen and straighten them, transforming V-shaped river valleys into U-shaped valleys.

- The valley floor is often covered with glacial debris and the sides are steep due to the ice's erosive force.

• Example : Yosemite Valley in California, USA

### HANGING VALLEY

- Hanging valleys are tributary valleys that are elevated above the main U-shaped valley.

- They form when smaller glaciers, unable to erode as deeply as larger glaciers, join the main glacial valley, leaving the tributary valley “hanging” above the main valley.

- These valleys often have waterfalls cascading from their mouths into the main valley below.

### CIRQUE / CORRIE

- Cirques are chair - shaped, bowl-like depressions found at the head of a glacial valley. They are formed by the erosive action of a glacier as it plucks and abrades the rock beneath it.

- They are formed as the snow accumulates and transforms into ice and the glacier erodes the hollow depression making it deeper and steeper.

- They typically have steep back walls and a flat or gently sloping basin.

### HORNS ( PYRAMIDAL PEAKS )

- Horns are sharp, pyramid-shaped peaks formed by the headward erosion of multiple cirques that surround a central point.

- It is formed when the three or more cirques erode a mountain summit from different sides, until their cirques meet high, sharp pointed and steep sided peaks called horns.

### ARETES

- Aretes are sharp, knife - edged ridges formed between two adjacent cirques or glacial valleys.

- They are created by the erosional activity of two glaciers eroding parallel valleys. The intense erosion on both sides narrows the ridge between them.

### TARN LAKE

- A lake of water can be seen quite often within the cirques after the glacier disappears, known as “Tarn or cirque lakes”

- The series of multiple glacial lakes connected by a stream or a braided stream system is termed a “PATER NOSTER LAKE”.

### DEPOSITIONAL

Depositional glacial landforms are the result of glaciers transporting and depositing sediments as they advance and retreat. These landforms are shaped by exogenic forces acting through glacial transportation and deposition of debris, which glaciers pick up from the underlying rock surface and surrounding landscape. The debris includes till, moraines, erratics and outwash, which contribute to the formation of distinct landforms.

![Geomorphic Processes – Figure 1](https://cdn-mineru.openxlab.org.cn/result/2026-09-02/d3afdc39-d85b-4dcd-bb42-06e7df48cde5/3953bed751d309f26178947309f0eed4897aed065ee680a7c3fe6a3952c1c365.jpg)

- Moraines are accumulations of glacial debris (till) that form ridges or mounds at different points around a glacier. They are the most common depositional landforms created by glaciers.

- Lateral Moraines : are formed along the sides of glaciers from debris that falls onto the glacier from surrounding slopes.

- Medial Moraines : form where two glaciers merge and their lateral moraines combine, creating a ridge of debris running down the center of the glacier.

- Terminal (End) Moraines : form at the farthest point of a glacier's advance. These ridges mark the maximum extent of the glacier.

- Ground Moraines : are formed as glaciers retreat and leave behind a blanket of till that covers the landscape.

### ESKERS

- Eskers are long, winding ridges of sand and gravel deposited by subglacial streams flowing beneath a glacier. These meltwater streams carry sediment, which gets deposited in the channels beneath the ice.

- Eskers can be several kilometers long and often resemble raised riverbeds or ridges, tracing the path of former subglacial [[Rivers]].

- Example: The Munro Esker is Canada's longest esker, stretching over 250 kilometers.

### KAME TERRACES

- Kame terraces are small, irregularly shaped hills or mounds of sand, gravel and till deposited by meltwater in depressions on the glacier's surface or along its margins.

- They form as sediment gets deposited in meltwater lakes or channels within or on top of the glacier. When the glacier melts, the sediments are left as mounds.

### KETTLE HOLES

- When a glacier retreats, chunks of ice can break off and become buried in till. As the ice melts, the overlying sediment collapses, forming a depression that fills with water.

- Kettle lakes are often circular and vary in size, depending on the size of the ice block that melted.

### DRUMLINS

- Drumlins are smooth, elongated hills formed by the deposition of glacial till. They have a smooth oval shaped ridge - like feature, with one end facing the glacier called ‘stoss’ and the other narrow end pointing ‘tail’ in the direction of glacier movement.

- Drumlins give an indication of direction of glacier movement.

### OUTWASH PLAINS

- Outwash plains are broad, flat areas of stratified sediment deposited by meltwater streams flowing from the glacier. The sediment is carried away from the glacier and deposited in layers.

- Outwash plains are usually located in front of the terminal moraine and are characterized by their flat, expansive nature.
